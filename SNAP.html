SNAP Benefit Risk Analysis

Identifying the characteristics of food-insecure households to direct support where it is most needed during periods of financial stress.

Overview

SNAP (formerly Food Stamps) is a critical lifeline for households that struggle to cover grocery costs. Economic downturns hit certain communities harder — and those same communities tend to recover more slowly. This project assessed demographics vulnerable to food insecurity using SNAP data from the USDA, conducting a 10-year gap analysis comparing 2007 and 2017 datasets to examine the lasting impacts of the 2008 housing crisis.

The analysis focused on two contrasting states identified through prior GIS spatial analysis: New Mexico (an emerging hot spot) and Nebraska (an emerging cold spot).

Methodology

Each dataset started with 40,000+ rows and 800 columns. Through correlation analysis, nullity assessment, domain knowledge, and Python optimization scripts, the data was reduced to 33 columns and ~4,000 final records.

  • Downloaded SNAP household data from the USDA for 2007 and 2017
  • Performed nullity assessment and dropped columns with high missingness
  • Used correlation analysis and domain knowledge to reduce to 33 relevant features
  • Extracted and compared state-level data for New Mexico and Nebraska
  • Built a Vote Ensemble model combining Random Forest, Gradient Boost, and Bagging Classifier
  • Achieved a 95% cross-validation score on the final model
  • Visualized feature importances and state-level predictions using Matplotlib and Seaborn
  • Mapped 2018 predictions spatially using QGIS and published as a webmap

Key Findings

Several surprising patterns emerged from the analysis:

  • SNAP requesters typically had small households — one to two children maximum
  • Approximately 50% were single mothers heading households
  • The top 4 biggest predictors of a household requesting SNAP were related to housing cost and stability
  • New Mexico showed greater economic vulnerability than Nebraska across both years
  • 2017 showed fewer working poor but more available assistance programs compared to 2007
